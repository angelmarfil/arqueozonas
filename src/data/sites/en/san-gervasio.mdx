### Description
This pre-Hispanic settlement, located on a former cattle ranch, was named in the 20th century after the patron saint of its
owner. As in many cases, the original Maya place name is unknown, although the site was recorded long ago.

### Historical overview
San Gervasio is the largest and most important site on Cozumel and, to date, the only one that has been the subject of a
permanent program of archaeological conservation and research. The reasons this location was chosen for the island's largest
settlement become obvious when one notes that it lies over the largest and most permanent aquifer, which surely guaranteed the
subsistence of its inhabitants. Archaeological work shows that the pre-Hispanic settlement was founded around 300 AD, when it
was a small settlement, although already the largest on the island. Later, around the year 600, the city began a period of rapid
growth, evidenced by the appearance of perishable architecture of better construction quality and by a possible strengthening
of its ties with the coastal sites of northern Quintana Roo. From the year 1000 onward, with the rise of Chichén Itzá as the
great political and economic center of the peninsula, the site became part of an increasingly intense economic network, which
would lead it to become the great city it was from 1200 AD. During the Late Postclassic (1200 – 1450 AD), most of the buildings
visible today were built, and a network of smaller communities dependent on San Gervasio was established across the whole
island. The site is made up of several architectural groups linked by a network of sacbés, or white roads. Its main groups are
known as Manitas, Chichán Nah ("Little House"), El Álamo, Nohoch Nah ("Great House"), Murciélagos, Ka'na Nah ("Tall House") and
El Ramonal, the latter still being explored and not open to visitors. Spanish chroniclers refer to the various Maya settlements
that existed on Cozumel when they arrived, linking them to the cult of the goddess Ixchel, patron of medicine, childbirth and
weaving. However, these references correspond to a now-destroyed site located where the present-day town of San Miguel de
Cozumel stands, home to the oracle dedicated to this goddess. No archaeological evidence linking San Gervasio to the cult of
Ixchel has been found.

### Location
It is located seven kilometers east of the present-day town of San Miguel de Cozumel and is easily reached via the island's
cross-island highway, a paved road in good condition built in 1972. Many travel agencies organize guided tours, and the local
taxi service offers the trip from the city's main hotels.

### Fees and services
Admission $85. Note that, in addition to the INAH entrance fee, the Cozumel Parks and Museums Foundation charges an extra fee
for use of the access road and the services area.

Parking, restrooms, craft shops and a snack bar run by a local foundation; INAH has a ticket office. There is an association of
certified guides.
